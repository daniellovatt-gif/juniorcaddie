Does my child
actually need
golf shoes?

Golf shoes are one of the easiest things to overspend on for a growing child — and one of the easiest to skip entirely for longer than most parents realise. Here's when they matter, what to buy, and what to ignore.

JuniorCaddie says
Don't buy golf shoes for a child who's had three lessons. Clean, comfortable trainers with reasonable grip do the job at Stage 1 — save the spend for when they're actually out on a course regularly.
The main decision
Spiked vs spikeless
Spikeless is the right default for almost every junior golfer in the UK.
Spikeless
  • Lighter and more comfortable for a still-developing gait
  • Doubles as a normal outdoor shoe — better value for a child who plays occasionally
  • No spike replacement cost as they wear
  • Grip is genuinely good enough for the vast majority of junior swing speeds and course conditions
Spiked
  • Worth it for a competitive junior regularly on wet, hilly or links courses
  • More stable at higher swing speeds — relevant from Stage 4 onward
  • Not a beginner requirement — buying spikes early is a common overspend
  • Some club dress codes still specify spikes for competitions — check before assuming
Worth the extra cost?
Waterproof or not

Genuine waterproofing adds cost, and for a Stage 1–2 junior playing occasionally in decent weather, it's rarely worth it — wet feet on an occasional wet day isn't a real problem. It becomes worth paying for once a child is playing regularly through a UK winter, or entering competitions that go ahead regardless of ground conditions, because a child who's uncomfortable and cold plays worse and enjoys it less. Most junior-specific ranges (rather than adult shoes sized down) build this in appropriately for the price point, so it's less about hunting for a specific feature and more about not buying the cheapest option once weather-in-all-conditions golf actually starts.

Golf shoes are one of the worst things to overspend on for a growing child — the foot that fits perfectly in August is often a size out by the following spring.
The part parents get wrong
Sizing, growth room, and how long they'll last

Buy with genuine growth room, not a snug fit. Golf shoes are worn for a whole round rather than short bursts, so a size that's slightly generous is far more forgiving than the tight fit you might choose for a school shoe.

Expect one pair to last roughly a season, sometimes less during the fastest growth years (typically Stage 1–3). That's normal, not a sign of poor quality — it's the same story as junior clubs. Budget for it rather than being caught out.

Barely-worn pairs hold real resale value precisely because juniors grow out of them before wearing them out — check the JuniorCaddie marketplace before buying new, and list outgrown pairs there rather than letting them sit in a cupboard.

Where to look
Brands with genuine junior fits

The junior golf shoe market is smaller than the junior clubs or clothing market, and fewer brands build a true junior-specific last rather than simply sizing an adult shoe down. FootJoy is the clearest example in the UK market — its junior range (sizes 1–6) is built as a dedicated line rather than a scaled-down adult shoe, with waterproof spikeless models as the mainstay. We cover FootJoy in more detail, alongside the rest of its junior range, in the clothing guide.

Several multi-sport brands (Skechers Go Golf, Under Armour, Nike, adidas) also sell junior or youth-sized golf shoes, though availability and true junior-specific sizing varies more than with a specialist. Worth trying in-store where possible, particularly for a child at the wider or narrower end of standard sizing.
