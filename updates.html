Stage One: First Swings

Before handicaps, before opens, before any of it, Stage One is simply about falling in love with the game. Get this bit right and everything else gets easier.

Why this stage matters more than any other

It's tempting to think of Stage One as the "easy bit" before the real development starts. In practice, it's the stage that decides whether there's a Stage Two at all. Juniors who fall in love with golf here — through games, freedom, and small wins — tend to stick with it through the harder stages later. Juniors who get pushed too hard, too early, often don't.

There's no fixed age for this. Some juniors arrive at Stage One at five, others at eleven. What matters isn't when they start — it's how it feels while they're there.

Coaches often describe this as the "sampling" phase — trying the game out, at low intensity, with no expectation attached. Golf has a longer sampling phase than most sports, because unlike football or swimming, a junior doesn't need a team, a fixture list, or even a lesson to start. A pitching wedge, a bucket of balls, and twenty free minutes is genuinely enough.

What Stage One actually looks like

  • Fun comes first, always. Short sessions — twenty minutes is plenty at this age. Games on the range beat drills every time: closest to the flag, longest drive, target chipping.
  • No scorecards yet. Counting shots too early turns exploration into judgement. There's plenty of time for medal cards later — right now it's about contact, not counting.
  • Chipping and putting first. The short game is where juniors get the fastest feedback and the quickest wins — full swing can wait.
  • Other sports stay in the mix. Juniors who play multiple sports at this age tend to develop better all-round coordination — and are less likely to burn out on any single one, golf included.
  • Parents set the tone. A relaxed, low-stakes start at this stage tends to carry through every stage that follows — the habits (and the pressure, or lack of it) set here are sticky.
A trap worth knowing about early
It's tempting, once a junior shows promise, to accelerate — more lessons, a full set instead of a half set, talk of "getting them into competitions soon." The juniors who go the distance are rarely the ones who moved fastest through Stage One. They're the ones whose parents let the stage run its natural course, however long that turned out to be. There's no prize for reaching Stage Three early.

A quick word on kit

Stage One doesn't need much — a half-set sized to their height is plenty, and it's genuinely one of the areas where second-hand makes the most sense, since they'll outgrow it fast. Sizing should always be by height, never by age — a well-fitted set makes contact easier and more rewarding, which matters far more at this stage than club quality.

Stage Two: Building the Game

Once a junior has fallen for the game, Stage Two is where it starts to take shape: first proper lessons, first fitted clubs, first junior section.

Why this stage is where habits get built

If Stage One is about falling in love with golf, Stage Two is about learning to actually play it. This is where fundamentals get built — grip, stance, the basics of a repeatable swing — and where a junior moves from hitting balls on a range to genuinely playing the game.

It's also where golf usually becomes social. Joining a junior section means training alongside other kids, not just a parent — coaches report this is often the single biggest driver of whether a junior sticks with the game long-term. Golf stops being a thing they do with you and starts being a thing they do for themselves.

There's a temptation at this stage to measure progress — handicaps, scores, comparisons with other juniors in the section. A little structure is healthy. Too much, too soon, and Stage Two starts to feel like Stage Three arrived early, which rarely goes well.

What Stage Two actually looks like

  • First proper lessons. Regular sessions with a PGA pro, working on grip, stance and the basics of a repeatable swing — the fundamentals that everything later gets built on.
  • Joining a junior section. Group coaching, other kids at a similar stage, and the first sense of golf as something social rather than solitary.
  • A properly fitted set. The half-set from Stage One usually gets upgraded here — still height-fitted, but built for someone playing regularly rather than just having a go.
  • First real holes. Playing on the course itself, not just the range — usually a few holes at a time, with a parent or coach, learning etiquette and pace of play in context.
  • Scoring enters gently. Not competition yet — just an awareness that shots are being counted, usually introduced through the club's junior section rather than anything formal.
A trap worth knowing about early
It's easy to start coaching from the sideline once you understand a bit of what the pro is teaching — a well-meant tip on the way home, a correction mid-round. It rarely lands the way it's intended. Juniors at this stage need one voice for technical instruction, and a parent's job is everything around that: turning up, encouraging, and letting the coach coach. Save the golf conversation for what they enjoyed, not what they got wrong.

A quick word on kit

This is usually when the first real upgrade happens — from a starter half-set to something built for regular play. Still always fitted by height, not age, but now it's worth thinking about a fuller set as lessons progress and more clubs come into use.

Stage Three: Getting Competitive

This is the stage where golf starts keeping score for real: first handicap, first opens, first proper nerves on the first tee.

Why this stage changes the relationship with the game

Stages One and Two were about falling for golf and learning to play it. Stage Three is where it starts to matter in a different way — a handicap gets registered, an entry form gets filled in, and for the first time there's a number attached to how the round went. That shift is bigger than it looks.

Done well, this is where junior golf gets genuinely exciting — the first-tee nerves, the first good finish, the first "I can't wait for the next one." Done badly, it's where golf can start to feel like a test rather than a game, and that's usually where juniors quietly drift away.

The difference between the two rarely comes down to talent. It usually comes down to pacing — how many competitions, how much scrutiny, and how much room is left for it to still be fun.

What Stage Three actually looks like

  • A first official handicap. Usually registered through the club or county union once enough qualifying rounds are in. It's a number, not a verdict — worth saying that to a junior more than once.
  • First real competitions. Club junior opens, county events, maybe a regional tour stop — entered deliberately now, not just played as extra holes on the way home.
  • Learning the entry system. BRS, HowDidiDo, intelligentgolf, county portals — every organiser seems to use something different. It's confusing at first for every parent, not just yours.
  • Scoring formats start to matter. Stableford, strokeplay, medal — understanding the format before teeing off changes how a junior plays the round, not just how it's scored afterwards.
  • Balancing golf with everything else. School, other sports, friends, downtime. The juniors who last are rarely the ones playing the most golf — they're the ones with the most balanced week.
A trap worth knowing about early
Once a junior can enter competitions, it's tempting to enter all of them — every county open, every regional tour stop, every weekend booked solid. A packed summer of golf sounds like commitment. More often it's the fastest route to burnout. Juniors who play three well-chosen events a month tend to stay hungrier, and improve faster, than juniors playing every single week. Leave room for golf to still feel like a choice, not a schedule.

A word on WHS and general play cards

Once that first handicap is in, it's tempting to keep submitting general play cards from every relaxed weekend round to try to bring the number down quickly. It's worth resisting that urge. General play scores, posted outside the pressure of an actual competition, don't always reflect how a junior performs when it counts — and a handicap that drops faster than the game underneath it can leave them under-clubbed against the field.

The better path is usually to let the handicap come down through competition rounds themselves, once that first number is established. It moves a little slower, but it keeps the handicap honest — and an honest handicap is what keeps a junior genuinely competitive in Stableford events, rather than finding the points just don't add up the way they expect on the day.

On good days and bad days

This is the stage where the highs and lows really start — a brilliant back nine one week, a card that falls apart the next. Our Competition Day Guide covers how to handle both, so a tough round doesn't shape whether they want to play another one.

A quick word on kit

Most of the kit decisions are already made by Stage Three — a fitted full set, decent shoes, a glove that actually fits. What's worth checking now is whether the set still fits: growth spurts are common at this age, and clubs that were right last year can quietly become wrong this year without anyone noticing until scores start to suffer for no obvious reason.

Stage Four: County & Beyond

Trials, squads, Order of Merit series and the wider pathways they open onto — Stage Four is where junior golf can start to feel like a genuine pathway rather than a series of one-off events.

Why this stage changes the game again

Stage Three was about entering competitions off your own initiative. Stage Four often starts with someone else's decision — a trial, a squad list, a county call-up. That's a real shift: for the first time, being good enough to play isn't entirely in the junior's control, and that takes some getting used to for the whole family.

Done well, this is where junior golf starts to feel like a genuine pathway — an Order of Merit points total building across a season, a county vest, training days alongside juniors from clubs they'd never otherwise have met. It's also usually where the logistics get harder: more fixtures, more travel, more portals to juggle between club, county and national bodies.

Selection, by its nature, means not everyone gets picked every time. How that's handled — by parents as much as coaches — tends to matter more here than at any stage so far.

What Stage Four actually looks like

  • County trials and squads. A first trial, training days alongside county-picked juniors, and the step up in standard that comes with it.
  • Order of Merit series. Season-long county leaderboards where points, not just one round, decide the standing — Sussex, Kent, Surrey and others all run these.
  • Regional and national pathways open up. Home Internationals, England Golf regional squads, national tour stops like PING JGT, Faldo and BJGT start to feel reachable rather than aspirational.
  • More travel, more portals. BRS, HowDidiDo, county union entry systems, national tour sign-ups — the logistics multiply as the geography widens.
  • Selection isn't guaranteed. Being good enough one month doesn't guarantee a place the next. Learning to handle a "not this time" is as much a part of this stage as making the squad.
A trap worth knowing about early
Once a junior is trialling for county squads, it's tempting to treat every selection as a verdict — on ability, on effort, on whether it's "worth it." It rarely is one. Squads are picked on the day, against whoever else turns up, for a specific number of spots. The juniors who keep progressing tend to be the ones whose parents treat a trial as one data point among many, not the whole story. There's no version of junior golf where every trial goes your way — the resilience that builds from the ones that don't is arguably more useful long-term than the ones that do.

A word on club, county and country

It helps to know roughly how the ladder works before a junior starts climbing it. Club junior sections are where most juniors play most of their golf. County squads are picked from club players, usually through an open trial or an invitation based on performance and handicap. From there, county performance can lead to regional or England Golf representative honours, and eventually the national tours, where the strongest juniors from across the country compete against each other rather than just their own county.

Every junior's route through it looks different, and most never go past county level — which is exactly as it should be. Stage Four is still meant to be enjoyed, not just endured on the way to somewhere else.

On the ups and downs of competition

At Stage Four, results start to carry more weight than before — an Order of Merit position, a squad place, a ranking against juniors from right across the county. That makes the highs feel bigger, and the lows sting more, than they did back at Stage Three's first nerves on the first tee. Trial days bring their own version of this: the outcome isn't entirely down to how the round goes, and it can be hard for a junior — or a parent — to separate a good performance from a good result.

None of that is a problem to be solved. Ups and downs are the game working as intended — the same unpredictability that makes a good round feel so good is what makes a bad one sting. What matters most is not letting either one define the week.

What parents can do

Ask about the round before the score. What they enjoyed, what they'd do differently, what surprised them — leading with that instead of the number keeps the conversation about golf, not just results.

Treat one result as one data point. A dropped Order of Merit place or a trial that doesn't go their way isn't a verdict on ability — the juniors who keep progressing usually have parents who treat any single result as one among many.

Agree what "a good day" looks like beforehand. Something unrelated to the result — hitting more fairways, staying calm after a bad hole — gives a junior something to be proud of whichever way the scorecard, or the squad list, goes.

Watch your own reaction first. Juniors read a parent's face at the scoreboard before they've processed a result themselves — calm from the sideline travels further than anything said afterwards.

Keep some golf pressure-free. Not every round needs to count towards an Order of Merit or a trial — a relaxed game with friends keeps golf feeling like a choice, not a running scoreboard.

A quick word on kit

By Stage Four, most of the core equipment decisions are already made — but travelling to more fixtures and courses tends to bring a few new questions: a proper stand bag rather than a carry strap, ball choice as scoring starts to matter more, and making sure gloves and grips are replaced before they wear thin rather than after.

Stage Five: Transition & College Golf

The junior chapter is closing — adult equipment, adult competition, and the biggest decisions of the whole journey so far.

Why this stage carries more weight than any before it

Stages One to Four were about falling for golf, learning to play it, and learning to compete. Stage Five is different — it's not about getting better at golf any more, it's about deciding what golf is actually for. Equipment moves to adult specification. Competition moves into adult amateur golf. And for the first time, there's a genuinely serious question on the table: what happens next?

The most important thing a family can do at this stage is have honest conversations about the pathway. There are two genuinely strong routes worth weighing up side by side: American college golf, and training as a PGA Professional through The PGA's own UK qualification. Neither is the "main" option with the other as a fallback — they suit different players and different circumstances, and a fully professional playing career is a third question worth its own honest answer too.

Most families we speak to have only ever heard of one of these routes. Both deserve a proper look before either gets ruled in or out.

What Stage Five actually looks like

  • Precise yardages become a baseline, not an advantage. Every serious amateur and professional knows their carry distance to within two yards, for every club — including wedges at half, three-quarter and full swing.
  • Two genuinely strong pathways, weighed up properly. American college golf and training as a PGA Professional in the UK both offer a serious, structured route forward — neither is the "main" option with the other as a backup.
  • WAGR enters the conversation. For families exploring US recruitment, the World Amateur Golf Ranking is the number coaches check first — and most UK parents have never heard of it until now.
  • The mental side gets harder before it gets easier. Recruiting emails, adult competition, exam pressure and comparison with equally serious players all land at once. A dip in motivation here is normal, not a warning sign.
  • The parent's role changes fundamentally. From logistics and lift-sharing to something closer to a trusted advisor — available when asked, quiet when not.
Two strong pathways, not one default and one backup
American college golf gets most of the airtime in UK junior golf circles — but training as a PGA Professional through The PGA's own qualification is every bit as serious a route, and it deserves the same level of research and consideration. A US scholarship depends on landing an offer from a specific coach in a specific recruiting year. The PGA pathway works differently — no upper age limit, a wage from day one, and a UK-based degree-level qualification. The right fit depends on the player in front of you, not on which pathway happens to be better known.

American college golf

The United States college golf system is one of the most significant opportunities available to a talented British junior golfer — and one of the least understood. A full or substantial athletic scholarship, four years of structured coaching and competitive golf far beyond anything available in UK university golf, and a degree from a recognised American institution. NCAA recruiting officially opens on 1 June of a player's 15th birthday year — the families who navigate it successfully almost always start researching at 14 or 15.

The PGA Professional pathway

Training to become a PGA Professional isn't a fallback for players who don't make it to America — it's a genuine, degree-level qualification held by over 8,500 members working in more than 80 countries. Trainees are employed and paid at a golf facility from day one, study part-time over three years towards a real qualification (or full-time via the BSc Applied Golf Management Studies at the University of Birmingham), and stay competitive throughout rather than fitting golf around lectures. There's no upper age limit, and courses are available on standard UK student finance. We spoke directly with The PGA's Student Recruitment team while building this section of the guide — worth a proper read via PGA Qualification Courses, starting an application, or the PGA's own Be Inspired stories.

A word on WAGR

A handicap tells a coach how good a player is on paper. WAGR — the World Amateur Golf Ranking, run jointly by The R&A and the USGA — tells them how that player actually performs against ranked opposition, and it's usually the number a US college coach checks first. A player isn't even visible on the list until they earn 6.5 points in a single strong event, which is the single most common reason a genuinely talented UK junior never appears on a recruiter's radar.

A quick word on kit

This is the most significant equipment investment of the entire junior journey. A proper custom fitting matters more here than at any earlier stage — wedge specification, driver shaft selection and putter fitting all deserve individual attention from someone who understands the specific challenges of the junior-to-adult transition.

Sharing the Journey · Goal Setting

1,000 Followers, Or a Good Tempo?

First published 13 September 2026 — a Journey extra, sitting alongside the five stages above.

A trend we've noticed among some junior golfers and can't quite decide what to make of: posting season goals, in order — hit 1,000 followers, beat a target score, be a good person on the course. Not all of them, and maybe not even most — but enough that it caught our eye. Genuinely curious what you make of it — get in touch, because we keep going back and forth.

The Pattern, And Why The Order Matters

It's showing up on some junior golf accounts right now — not all of them, not even most — but often enough to notice: a caption listing "goals for the season," and it opens with a follower count. Then a scoring target. Then, almost as an afterthought, something about sportsmanship or being a good person on the course.

The follower number itself doesn't seem like the interesting part — plenty of juniors enjoy building an audience, and there's a reasonable case that it's just how this generation talks about ambition. The part worth debating is the order. Does listing character last actually mean anything, or are we reading too much into where three sentences happen to fall in a caption? There's a case both ways.

The Case For Taking The Order Seriously

  • It's entirely outside their control. A scoring goal is earned through practice and decisions on the course. A follower count is earned through an algorithm, other people's scrolling habits, and luck of timing.
  • It measures attention, not performance. A junior can shoot the round of their life and gain no followers, or shoot a poor round and post something that goes viral for unrelated reasons.
  • It ties self-worth to something contingent. Young athletes who tie their sense of value to external validation tend to show more anxiety and less resilience after a bad performance than those who tie it to effort or improvement.
  • It rewards the wrong content. Trick shots and highlight reels generate followers. Grinding out a two-hour practice session on a weak short game does not.

Or Maybe It's The Wrong Thing To Focus On Entirely

Here's the counterargument: sportsmanship and honesty aren't really "goals" in the same sense as a scoring target — they're more like a baseline, the entry price for playing at all. If that's true, then listing them last isn't a warning sign, it's just a category error that doesn't mean much. So is this actually revealing something about priorities — or are we over-reading three lines in an Instagram caption?

One Framework, For What It's Worth

Sport psychology usually splits goals into three tiers: process goals (specific, controllable actions — "commit fully to every pre-shot routine"), performance goals (personal standards — a scoring average, a handicap target), and outcome goals (results that depend partly on others — winning an event, a ranking). A follower count doesn't sit neatly in any of those three.

How The Pros Actually Set Goals

Since we're floating a framework, it's worth checking it against how the actual best golfers alive describe their own goal-setting — because none of them, it turns out, lead with an outcome.

Three Golfers, Zero Outcome Goals

Scottie Scheffler has said plainly he isn't "super goal-oriented" and would rather stay present than chase a number. Before the 2024 Olympics, his stated aim wasn't to win gold — he won it anyway.

Rory McIlroy works with sports psychologist Dr. Bob Rotella, setting "performance goals" broken into "process goals" entirely within his control, like a target percentage of fairways hit. He's said he moved away from writing down major wins once he recognised how much of that he couldn't control.

Tiger Woods, at the 2005 Masters with his father seriously ill, channelled the desire to win into one process goal — good tempo, on every shot. He won the tournament.

Three very different golfers, three different eras — and not one of them leads with a number they can't control. Make of that what you will.

Good morning, and what a week to be picking up a scorecard. Final-hole birdies, a playoff that went right down to the wire, and a fresh crop of county champions heading for Woodhall Spa in September — junior golf delivered on every front that week.

Headline — Gog Magog, Cambridge

Brooks holes the winning birdie as Jimenez survives a playoff thriller. It doesn't get much more dramatic than this. At the English Girls' Under-14 & Under-16 Open Amateur Championships, England's Maybel Brooks produced the shot of the week — a birdie on the very last hole — to snatch the Under-14 title in style. One group and one age category up, the Under-16 crown needed extra holes to settle: Spain's Paula Jimenez came through a tense playoff against Germany's Lorena Guth to take the title.

It could easily have gone England's way at the top too — home hope Selina Bartaya led the Under-16 field after round one, only to be reeled in by Germany's Jinlei Zhong as the tournament wore on. In the Under-14s, Italy's Elena Sartori pushed hard and finished third, keeping Brooks honest right to the closing stretch.

County Round-Up

Two counties wrapped up their junior championships that week, both sending fresh names through to the England Golf Champion of Champions at Woodhall Spa — one of the 410+ hand-verified competitions on the JuniorCaddie competition finder.

Kent Junior Championship — Prince's Golf Club

Boys: Tommy Hulance (Dartford) ran away with it, opening with a five-under 67 and holding on for a 140 total — five clear of the field.

Girls: Selina Bartarya (Royal Blackheath) was in a different class, backing up a 73 with a superb 69 to win by ten shots on 142.

Hertfordshire Girls County Championship — Old Fold Manor

Amelia Parker (Letchworth) claimed the county title with a gross 83, winning by three shots. In the handicap section, Mira Pasura (Moor Park) took the honours on a net 70. Parker now carries Hertfordshire's flag to Woodhall Spa in September.

One to Watch

Down in the South West, early word from the West of England Under-18 Open at Burnham & Berrow pointed to strong performances from England internationals including Arianna Virik — final scores weren't confirmed at the time of writing.

This week we're leading with something a bit different — a conversation that's been building across junior golf parent circles, and one we think is worth adding our voice to. Alongside it: reassurance that October's calendar is far from thin, five titles in a row for England's juniors internationally, and a new junior tour recruiting partnership.

Headline — Are handicap ballots quietly shutting juniors out of the biggest events?

Andy from Father Son Golf (@father_son_golf) posted a series this week that's struck a nerve with junior golf parents, and we think it deserves wider attention. Using England Golf's own published ballot data, he tracked the entry-handicap cutoff for three of the country's leading junior championships over the last three years — and the trend is stark.

Three ballots, three years, one direction

McGregor Trophy: 1.6 (2024) → 1.1 (2025) → 0.2 (2026).

Reid Trophy: 5.5 (2025) → 4.7 (2026).

Carris Trophy (plus-handicap ballot): +0.9 (2024) → +1.6 (2025) → +2.0 (2026) — meaning it now takes a considerably better player just to get a starting place.

His point isn't just about the numbers falling — it's about what's driving them. England Golf's own Championship Entry Policy allows an entry to be refused if a handicap record contains more than four General Play scores, fewer than four Competition scores, or is otherwise incomplete — a rule designed to keep ballots reflective of genuine ability. Andy's argument is that this now squeezes honest players out at the margins, at exactly the moment a place in these fields can matter for college recruitment and rankings, not just the trophy itself. As he put it: for a 15, 16 or 17-year-old trying to build their future through golf, one decimal point can be the difference between playing and watching from home — and that pressure is real.

Where we stand
We replied to the post directly, because this isn't new to us — it sparked an almighty debate among our own Hertfordshire Golf Organisers a few weeks back, and clubs are already responding inconsistently, with some now banning General Play scores from being submitted at all. We think the analysis holds, and we agree change is needed. But we'd add an uncomfortable truth of our own: despite these falling ballots, "on average" the players who do make these major fields aren't performing to the handicaps that got them in — which means opportunities are genuinely being taken from other juniors. Pressure has to be part of the picture, but that piece of analysis needs doing properly, not assumed.

Our proposal: a proper consultation, not a unilateral rule change. England Golf convenes the counties; the counties gather feedback from club junior organisers — and players themselves — first; and the outcome is national guidance everyone moves forward on together, rather than every club setting its own General Play policy in isolation. The alternative — locking Under-18s out of My England entirely and requiring every card to be registered through competitions or pro shops — is the nuclear option. A consultation should be the first step, not that.

It's also why this debate landed close to home for us. We've written about exactly this pattern before — Stage 3 of Our Journey has a section called "The general play trap", built around the same principle Father Son Golf's data is now showing at national scale: a healthy handicap is built mostly from competition rounds, with general play used sparingly to fill genuine gaps between them — not as the main source of scores. Submit too many relaxed evening cards and the index falls faster than the child's game actually has, and a junior ends up arriving at a competition off a number they've never really matched under pressure. That's a confidence problem at club level. At McGregor, Carris and Reid level, with real places on the line, it's the same gap — just with much higher stakes.

Update — momentum is building
A group of parents, junior organisers and coaches calling themselves the Junior Golf Integrity Initiative have gone further than a comment thread — they're now writing directly to county associations with a specific, narrower proposal: where an elite junior competition is oversubscribed, only competition scores should be used to determine the ballot, while General Play scores continue to serve their normal purpose everywhere else in the World Handicap System. Counties aren't being asked to take an official position, just to make sure clubs, organisers and coaches know the issue exists so people can decide for themselves. We think this is exactly the kind of concrete, workable proposal a consultation should be weighing — and we'll be adding our support. If you'd like to add yours too, their campaign form is open at forms.gle/7fx1incYkFL4k2jR8.

Father Son Golf is asking people to comment "CHANGE" on the post, or reach out by DM, to help build the conversation. If this resonates with you as a parent or organiser, it's worth adding your voice too.

Headline — A Competition We Hadn't Covered

Junior Team Golf (JTG) is a UK-wide club-team championship — not individual stroke play, but clubs fielding teams against each other. Over 50 Regional Qualifiers and Semi-finals run across England, Scotland and Wales in June/July and again in September/October — which means qualifying is happening right now — before national champions from all four home nations meet in the Home Nations Final. This year's was decided at Carlisle GC in April: England's Waterfront beat Scotland's Whitecraigs and Drumpellier, plus England runners-up Wrag Barn, to become the 31st holders of the Nigel Dolton Trophy.

The alumni list is the real hook: Matt Fitzpatrick, Grant Forrest, Georgia Hall and Charlie Hull all played JTG as juniors before reaching Walker, Ryder, Curtis or Solheim Cup level — elite players once lined up alongside "double-figure handicap teenage golfers," in the organisers' own words.
