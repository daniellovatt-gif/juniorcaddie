🎯 What this stage is really about

Stage 3 is the moment the game becomes real. A first handicap is a rite of passage — the formal recognition that this child is a golfer, measured and recorded within the structure of the game. For many juniors it is the first time they have a number that reflects their ability in any sport, and the emotional weight of that should not be underestimated.

Competition introduces pressures that practice and casual golf never can. Your child must now perform under scrutiny — from other players, from the scoreboard, often from parents watching. They will experience the full emotional range that golf provides: the elation of a birdie in competition, the frustration of a card-wrecking hole. How they learn to process these experiences will shape them not just as golfers but as people.

The transition from practice to competition is the biggest in junior golf. A child can be technically excellent on the range and fall apart on the course. The difference is almost always mental — and at Stage 3 the mental game is enormously influenced by what the parents do and say.

— PGA Junior Development Coach
Body & Readiness

🏃 Body & Readiness: the growth spurt

Stage 3 is when most children hit their fastest growth. The peak growth spurt typically comes at around 10–12 for girls and 12–14 for boys (London Bridge Sports Medicine), though every child is different. Parents should expect golf to feel harder for a while.

What you may notice:

  • A swing that worked in spring stops working by autumn. Longer limbs change timing. This is normal and temporary. Don't let it turn into a crisis or a swing rebuild.
  • Clubs suddenly too short. Recheck fit every few months during a growth spurt, not every six. See the sizing calculator and club reviews.
  • Tiredness and clumsiness. Growing bodies need more sleep and more food.
  • Aches around the knees or heels. Growth plates are more vulnerable during a spurt, especially with lots of running and jumping in other sports.

What to do:

  • Cut range-ball volume during a spurt, not golf itself. More short game and on-course play, fewer buckets of drivers.
  • Keep the warm-up and add a few minutes of gentle hip and trunk mobility.
  • Pain is a stop sign. If pain lasts more than a few days, changes how your child swings or walks, or wakes them at night, stop and see your GP or a physiotherapist. Don't play through it.

🗺️ Getting the competition balance right

One of the most common mistakes at Stage 3 is over-competing. A parent who enters their child in every available competition every weekend creates a schedule that leaves no time for practice, no time for rest, and no time to embed the technical changes their coach is working on. If every weekend is a competition, there is no time to develop a better game.

A well-structured Stage 3 season includes meaningful competition balanced with dedicated practice time, fun golf with friends and family, and genuine rest. Junior golf burnout at Stage 3 is real. Children who play 40+ competitive rounds a year at age 10 or 11 are significantly more likely to disengage from the game by their early teens.

How to Practise

🎮 How to Practise at Stage 3

Every session should have one focus and end with a test. A focus means one thing to improve. A test means a game with a score, so progress can be measured.

A typical week in season1 lesson, 2 practice sessions of 60–75 minutes, 9 holes on the course
Rough split of practiceAbout half short game, a third full swing, the rest on-course or "play" practice
On the courseAt least one practice round a week, with a goal that isn't score (e.g. "hit every green in the middle")
Keep it simpleTrack three numbers per round: putts, up-and-downs, penalty shots

A sample 60-minute session:

  1. 10 minutes: warm-up and easy swings.
  2. 20 minutes: today's focus from the coach, repeating the same shot to groove it.
  3. 20 minutes: the same skill with a different club or target every shot.
  4. 10 minutes: a scored test (for example, 10 chips from different lies, points for finishing inside a club length).
Course management

🗺️ Course management

The first taste of real strategy. Build on Stage 2's three ideas:

  • Know your "safe miss" on each hole: which side of the green or fairway is least costly.
  • Play to your full-swing distances, not your best-ever ones. Start logging carry distances with the yardage chart.
  • One bad hole is one bad hole. The job on the next tee is a normal, boring par or bogey.

⚠️ The general play trap

Under the World Handicap System, a junior can submit a general play score from almost any relaxed round with friends or family, alongside their competition rounds. Used well, this is a genuine benefit — it lets a developing golfer's handicap keep pace between competitions. Used carelessly, it creates a quiet problem that only shows up months later, on the first tee of a real event.

A general play card, holed out with mates on a quiet Tuesday evening, is rarely played under the same pressure as a county open or a club competition. Fewer nerves, a bit more generosity with gimmes and preferred lies, less riding on every shot. Submit enough of those scores and a handicap starts to reflect the calm evening round, not the child's game under pressure. The index falls — but the scoring ability that's supposed to back it up hasn't actually arrived yet.

The result is a child who arrives at a competition off a handicap of, say, 12, but who has never actually shot anything close to that number when it mattered. That gap between the number on the card and the score they can produce under real pressure is where confidence gets damaged — not because they've become a worse golfer, but because the number was never a fair reflection of the competitive game in the first place.

🔍
A simple rule of thumb

A healthy Stage 3 handicap is built mostly from competition rounds, with general play used sparingly to fill genuine gaps between them — not as the main source of scores. If your child's index is falling but their competition scores aren't following it downward at roughly the same rate, that's the signal to pause the general play submissions and let competition golf catch the handicap back up.

It's also worth talking to your child's coach or club about how many general play cards is reasonable for a junior at this stage. There's no single national number that fits every player, but the club professional will know what looks normal versus what looks like the handicap is running ahead of the game.

A handicap earned on a quiet Tuesday evening writes a cheque the child has to cash on competition Saturday. If the numbers don't match when it counts, it's rarely the child's golf that's the problem — it's how the handicap got there.

— PGA Junior Development Coach

🧠 The mental game: the first taste of pressure

First handicap, first competition, first scorecard with their name on it in front of other people. Stage 3 is where golf stops being a private activity and becomes something your child does in public, with a number attached. That shift is exciting — and it's also the moment fear of failure and comparison with other juniors typically show up for the first time. Neither is a problem to fix. Both are completely normal reactions to a completely new experience, and both respond well to a bit of structure.

Two things make the biggest difference at this stage: giving your child a simple routine they can lean on when nerves show up mid-round, and protecting the conversation you have with them afterwards. Research on junior sport consistently finds that a parent's tone and questions in the car home shape a child's relationship with competition more than almost anything a coach does.

🚦
Exercise: Red-Amber-Green
A simple three-step pre-shot routine: Red — stop and look at the shot. Amber — pick a target and imagine the shot. Green — commit and swing without a second thought. It's easy for a child to remember under pressure, and it gives nerves somewhere useful to go.
🌬️
Exercise: Box Breathing
Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4 — one round, standing still, before a tee shot that feels big. It's a genuine physiological reset, not just a distraction, and it takes about 16 seconds.
🚗
Exercise: Two Good Things
On the way home, ask your child to name two things that went well before either of you mentions the score. It trains their brain — and yours — to notice progress first, and keeps the post-round conversation something they look forward to rather than dread.
🪞
Exercise: Comparing to Yourself Only
When your child compares their score to a friend's, gently redirect to "how does that compare to your own last round?" It keeps the focus on genuine progress rather than a moving target of other children's results.

The three words that do the most damage after a junior round aren't cruel ones — they're "so, how'd you do?" asked the second a child gets in the car. Wait. Let them decompress. The score will still be there in five minutes, and the conversation will be a much better one.

— Chartered Sport Psychologist, Junior Golf

How to support your child at Stage 3

Your behaviour at competitions matters more than you know. Junior golf research consistently identifies parental behaviour during competition as one of the strongest predictors of whether a child continues to compete. Be quiet when your child is playing. React identically to a birdie and a bogey. Never discuss the scorecard during a round.

The debrief has a formula. Three positive observations before any area of improvement is discussed. Ask your child what they think first — they almost always know what went wrong. What they need from you is not analysis. It is stability.

Watch for signs of burnout. A child who is reluctant to go to golf, who complains of fatigue before competitions, who becomes emotional around competition days — these are warning signs. Talk to the coach. Consider reducing the schedule. The long-term relationship with the game is worth more than any short-term result.

Equipment at Stage 3
Quality and fitting both matter now.

A full set — driver, fairway wood or hybrid, irons through to sand wedge, putter. A proper club fitting with a PGA Professional experienced in junior fitting is now justified. Incorrect lie angles and shaft lengths at this level actively impede technique — the player compensates for the equipment.

Plan for the next resize before you need it. A rapidly growing Stage 3 junior with the wrong clubs for six months of the season is a false economy. Sell through JuniorCaddie promptly — demand for quality Stage 3 equipment is high.

Coach Checklist

✅ What good junior coaching looks like

A short checklist to judge a coach against.

The non-negotiables

✓
Qualified and safeguarded. A PGA Professional or a licensed volunteer coach, with a current enhanced DBS check and safeguarding certificate. Both appear on the published SafeGolf lists, run by the UK golf bodies including England Golf and The PGA (PGA safeguarding).
✓
Open sessions. Parents can watch, or at least see, every session. No coach should need a closed door with a child.
✓
Clear communication. You know what your child is working on and why.

Signs of a good junior coach

  • Asks your child questions, rather than only giving instructions
  • Uses games and targets, especially at Stages 1–2
  • Gives one thing to work on at a time
  • Has a plan across months, not just this lesson
  • Encourages other sports and rest
  • Talks about effort and decisions, not just scores
  • Tells you honestly where your child is, even when it isn't what you hoped

Warning signs

✕
Long technical lectures with young children
✕
A swing rebuild mid-season without explanation
✕
Pressure to buy equipment from one shop or brand
✕
Promises about county selection, scholarships or "the next Rory"
✕
Discouraging your child from other sports
✕
Criticism of your child in front of others

What's right at each stage

1Group sessions, games, lots of laughing. Technique almost invisible
2Groups plus the occasional individual lesson. Fundamentals hidden inside games
3A regular coach who comes to watch the occasional round and knows the competition calendar
4A coach who works with a strength and conditioning coach and plans the season with you
5A coach who treats the player as the decision-maker and helps with pathway choices
