🎯 What this stage is really about

Stage 4 is where junior golf becomes a serious pursuit. The child is training with intent, competing at county level or above, and investing significant time and energy in their development. For families, this stage brings new complexity — the competitive calendar is more demanding, the coaching is more specialised, and the emotional stakes are higher for everyone.

It is also the stage where the best coaches spend considerable time on something most families never discuss explicitly: identity. A junior at Stage 4 needs a healthy relationship with their performance — one where they understand they are not defined by their handicap, their county ranking, or their results. Players who tie their self-worth to their scores are fragile. Players who see golf as something they do rather than something they are — are resilient.

The players who make it from Stage 4 to elite amateur golf are rarely the most talented children at twelve. They are almost always the ones who stayed curious, stayed coachable, and stayed in love with the game when it was hard. Talent is the starting point. Character is the destination.

— PGA Junior Development Coach
Body & Readiness

🏃 Body & Readiness: conditioning and injury prevention

At Stage 4 the body starts to limit the game. Players have the technique to hit it further and practise more, and the body has to keep up. The two biggest risks are sudden jumps in practice volume and a body that can't rotate well.

Build a golf body, with supervision:

  • Two strength and mobility sessions a week, planned by a qualified strength and conditioning coach or physio who works with young athletes. Bodyweight and technique first; heavy loads only once technique is solid.
  • Focus on hips, trunk and balance. Research on golfers links low back pain with limited hip rotation and weak trunk muscles. A 2025 trial with adolescent golfers found a short pre-round routine improved lead-hip strength (Hamada et al., BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation).
  • A proper warm-up before every round and range session: hip and trunk rotation stretches, lunges with a turn, then gradually longer swings. About 10 minutes.
  • Speed training needs supervision. Swing-speed programmes are popular. Done badly, during growth, they load the back and wrists hard.

Watch the load:

  • The most common cause of overuse injury is a sudden jump, such as a summer camp after a quiet spring, or 500 balls in a day. Increase practice gradually.
  • Hitting lots of balls off hard mats is hard on wrists and elbows. Grass where possible.
  • Remember the age rule: weekly hours of all organised sport no more than their age, and under 16 hours a week.
  • Sleep and food are training. Growing teenagers need plenty of sleep and a proper breakfast before a 7am tee time.

Pain is a stop sign. Back pain that lasts more than a few days, pain that changes the swing, or pain at night: stop and see a GP or physiotherapist, preferably one with sport experience.

🏛️ The county pathway

Stage 4 is when most juniors engage seriously with the county structure for the first time. County squads, county championships, inter-county matches — these are environments that no club competition can replicate. The team element of inter-county golf introduces collective purpose and responsibility that is enormously valuable for a young player's development.

Selection for county squads varies between counties. The best preparation is consistent performance in county-organised events — the JuniorCaddie competition finder lists these across England. County coaches attend these events specifically to identify talent.

How to Practise

🎮 How to Practise at Stage 4

Practice now follows a plan agreed with the coach, built around the player's stats. The biggest gains usually come from the part of the game the player least enjoys practising.

A typical week in season1 lesson, 3–4 practice sessions of 60–90 minutes, 1–2 on-course sessions, 2 strength and mobility sessions
Practice mixSome technical work, most time on varied practice, and at least one pressure test per session
Stats to trackFairways, greens, up-and-downs, putts per round, penalty shots
YardagesExact carry distances for every club, from the yardage chart

🧠 The mental game: composure under real stakes

County trials, squad places, being ranked against players they now know by name — Stage 4 is where the stakes stop being abstract. Your child is old enough now to genuinely want a result, which means they're also old enough to genuinely fear losing it. This is exactly the age where the gap opens up between players with a strong technical game and players who can also hold themselves together when a round starts going wrong — and it's the second group who keep progressing.

This is also the age where the earliest signs of burnout tend to appear, often mistaken for a "bad patch" rather than what it actually is: a young player who has been all-golf, all the time, for long enough that the enjoyment has quietly drained out. Watching for this matters as much as building resilience.

📏
Exercise: The 10-Yard Rule
Give yourself the walk to your next shot — roughly 10 yards, or 10 seconds — to feel whatever you need to feel about the last one. When you arrive, it's gone. A hard boundary stops one bad hole becoming three.
✅
Exercise: The 3 Cs Check
Before a big shot, a three-word internal check: Confidence — have I hit this shot before? Concentration — am I fully here? Composure — is my breathing calm? Thirty seconds, entirely internal, usable in any competition.
📓
Exercise: Best-Worst-Next Journal
After every round: one thing that went well, one thing to work on, and one specific, process-based goal for the next round. It keeps a player's focus on controllable things rather than the final number.
🚩
Parent checklist: burnout warning signs
Watch for dread before events rather than nerves, flat or absent emotion after both good and bad rounds, and reluctance to practise without being asked. Any of these sustained for more than a few weeks is worth a genuine conversation about taking the foot off the pedal.

By the time a junior reaches proper county-level pressure, technique has usually stopped being the difference between players. What separates them is who can have a disaster on the 4th hole and still play the 5th like it's a fresh round.

— Chartered Sport Psychologist, Junior Golf

How to support your child at Stage 4

This is the stage where parental involvement most often becomes counterproductive. The stakes feel higher and it is natural to feel more emotionally engaged in outcomes. But a Stage 4 junior who is being managed too closely by their parents — whose coaching decisions are second-guessed, whose results are discussed too intensely — is a junior who is playing golf for their parents rather than for themselves.

Your role is logistics and emotional stability. You get them to training and competitions. You manage the schedule so they have enough rest and variety in their life. You are the same person after a 68 as after an 84. You trust the coach and the process.

Watch for identity fusion. If your child's mood and self-esteem become tightly linked to their golf results, this is a warning sign. A bad round should not ruin a weekend. The goal is perspective — golf is what they do, not who they are.

Equipment at Stage 4
Proper fitting is non-negotiable at this level.

A Stage 4 player should have been fitted by a PGA Professional with junior fitting experience. The variables at this level — shaft flex, lie angle, loft, grip size — have a direct and measurable impact on ball-striking. Off-the-shelf equipment, however expensive, will not perform as well as properly fitted equipment.

Many Stage 4 players begin transitioning toward adult-specification equipment. This should be managed with coach and fitter — it is not simply a matter of switching at a certain age. The right moment depends on physical development, swing speed, and technical maturity.

⚙️

📐 Knowing your yardages — non-negotiable at Stage 4

At Stage 4, guessing distances is no longer acceptable. A player who doesn't know how far they carry a 7-iron, a gap wedge or a driver is making every course management decision in the dark. Knowing your yardages precisely — not approximately — is one of the single biggest performance gains available at this level, and it costs nothing.

The standard at county level is exact carry distances for every club in the bag. Not "about 150 yards." Exactly 147 yards on a neutral day with no wind. This precision matters because course management decisions — lay-up distances, approach club selection, wedge distances around the green — are all built on it. A player who knows they carry their 52-degree wedge exactly 98 yards can position a lay-up shot with surgical accuracy. A player who thinks they carry it "about 100" is gambling every time.

Use a launch monitor session with your coach to establish precise carry yardages. Repeat this every four to six months — swing speed and distances change significantly during adolescent development. Then build a personal yardage card for your bag and refer to it on every approach shot.
